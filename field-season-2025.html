Field Season 2025: Mapping Invasives Across a Land Trust's Preserves

A summer and fall with a range-pole Trimble GPS and ArcGIS Field Maps, mapping invasive plants, trails and photo-monitoring points across 13 preserves in Washington, Ozaukee and Milwaukee counties for a land trust's grant-funded invasive species removal program.

The job

The land trust had just won a large grant to remove non-woody invasive plants from its preserves, and it needed to know where they were. Each morning I picked up a work phone running ArcGIS Field Maps and a Trimble GPS on a range pole (about 4-inch accuracy), and spent the day on foot.

In summer I mapped the non-woody invasives the grant targeted: white sweet clover, teasel, Canada thistle, crown vetch, bird's-foot trefoil, wild parsnip and spotted knapweed, into the trust's 2025 Invasives web map. In fall the work switched to woody invasives (buckthorn, autumn olive, honeysuckle, multiflora rose, Japanese barberry, black locust, Siberian elm) plus trail data. All season I also returned to photo-monitoring points first shot in 2020 and re-took them, so the trust could see how its old farm fields were changing.

Thanks to GPS, Field Maps and plant identification from my UW-Stevens Point labs and plant walks, I was trusted to map on my own from the first day, without the planned shadowing period. Some days I worked alone; others I walked with the stewardship crew and mapped what they were removing.

Points or polygons?

The rule I mapped by

A patch about 6 feet across or smaller is a point. Anything bigger is a polygon, recorded live by walking its edge with the GPS.

The two people using these maps wanted different things. The grant-writing side wanted detailed polygons, because precise acreage wins funding. The field stewards wanted fast points, just enough to find a patch again next season. Polygons take far longer to walk, so I settled on a balance: polygons where the size mattered for treatment and reporting, points everywhere else. It's the same question every field data collection plan has to answer: what does each user of the data actually need?

I didn't always get it right the first time. One September day at Forest Beach I worked zoomed in too far and logged dozens of points along what was really one continuous thicket of autumn olive and honeysuckle. My log for that day reads "too many points, need to rework into polygons," and I went back and did.

The hard part

This was harder than the invasives mapping. The 2020 points were collected with a GPS accurate to about 30 feet, against my 4 inches, so the recorded spot was never quite where the photo was taken. The old photos were also shot in June and mine in August, and succession had moved fast: fields that were open in 2020 were closing in with buckthorn and ash saplings, and some points couldn't be reached at all. I found each spot by matching the skyline, tree lines and landmarks in the old photos rather than trusting the coordinates.

From the field

Things I found

A rare wildflower

Royal catchfly, tucked into a forgotten corner of the Fellenz Woods prairie. It's sensitive to the prescribed fires used to maintain prairie, so it's rare on the preserves.

A driveway full of teasel

At Making Allies Farm, teasel had taken over the entrance, and the old house site had nearly every species on the list. I mapped the whole farm in one day, including a tangle of overlapping polygons where species grew mixed together.

A prairie that came back

The 160-acre Fellenz Woods prairie was once almost all white sweet clover. By the time I mapped it, only scattered red dots were left, and students from the Land Restoration School helped pull the last of them.
