Case study · Chapter 1 of 4 Remote sensing · Google Earth Engine Lake Winnebago, Wisconsin · 2017–2026

Lake Winnebago from Space

My grandmother said the west wind blew the algae to our side of the lake. I checked her claim against 250 clear days of satellite images and ten summers of wind. After a west wind, the east shore had more chlorophyll than the west on 80% of days.

Draft for review. Not yet linked from the homepage.

Why this lake

For generations my family has been coming to Lake Winnebago in the summer. Every year my extended family would stay at an uncle's cottage on the lake's east shore for nine days. Many sunny days that were perfect for swimming were cut short when mats of algae blew in from the other side of the lake. Swirling, marbled blues and greens formed a thick mat on the surface that smelled like sulfur. Swimming, kayaking and fishing were canceled. It was board game time for the rest of the week.

Those mats have been engraved in my mind for years. They were beautiful, and they stank. My grandma JoAnn always said, "The west wind blows the blue-green algae from the other side of the lake." But we were there for only nine days a year, which is a worm's-eye view of the problem. I wanted to check for myself whether she was right. So I went digging...

We learned what the wind does on Lake Winnebago long before I looked at any data. A south-southeast wind sent my cousin's kayak to the middle of the lake so fast he had to break the sail, and we went out in my uncle's motorboat to bring him in.

Result

Chlorophyll is higher on the downwind shore

I sorted 250 clear summer days, June 2017 to September 2026, by the direction the wind had come from over that day and the two before it. Then I compared chlorophyll-a in a 2 km strip along the east shore with the same strip along the west shore.

Reading it

A west wind is the strongest of the eight directions: the east shore came out higher on 80% of 50 days, by an average of 11.8 mg/m³. A northeast wind reverses it. The east shore was higher on only 25% of those 36 days.

So she was right about the wind. What the data shows is where the chlorophyll sits after the wind has blown, not algae in motion, and it does not show the bloom starting on the far side. The algae grows across the whole lake and the wind sorts it.

Reading it

July 25 and August 9 followed west winds and July 28 a southwest wind, and on all three the line between the two sides of the lake is sharp. On July 10 the wind had been from the south, along the length of the lake, and the red band sits toward the west side. By August 27 it had swung to the southeast, and the west shore was the higher one.

The red also spreads as the summer goes on. That is the whole lake blooming, not a patch moving west: the west shore rises from 28 to 61 across these weeks while the east stays high. The color scale stops at 60, so everything above it is the same red.

Reading it

Green streaks run down the east side in 2016, 2017 and 2018. The 2017 image, from August 5, sits between two Sentinel-2 days that measured the east shore at 2.6 and 4.6 times the west, so two different satellites agree on that week.

The vivid 2025 frame, from August 3, needs care. Sentinel-2 passed the day before and found only moderate chlorophyll, and the Landsat image has a pale haze patch across the middle. Some of that green is surface scum and some is haze and the way the image is brightened.

Checking it

Did anyone on the water see the same thing?

A satellite estimate is only worth as much as its match to what people saw and measured. Four checks: news and fishing reports, my own family's photos, the DNR's lab samples, and a USGS boat that measured blue-green algae directly.

Each report of a bad bloom lands on or beside a day when the satellite numbers are well above normal. The fishing report also describes something the wind test cannot see: strong wind stirs floating algae down into the water, so slicks form in calm weather and are moved by light to moderate wind.

My family's photos

Sentinel-2 passed on August 9 and August 12, 2018, either side of the weekend in the first two photos. On the 9th the lake median was 40 mg/m³ with the east shore at 50 and the west at 33. By the 12th the lake was down to 25. Winds were light both days, which matches the glassy water in the photo.

The dock photo is from three weeks later. The satellite saw only cloud on September 1 and 3, which fits the sky in the picture.

Reading it

The satellite estimate follows the boat's blue-green pigment, and it does not follow the boat's own chlorophyll channel (rank correlation −0.19). Blue-green algae give off little of the fluorescence that channel reads. So on this lake in summer, the chlorophyll number from space is mostly a measure of blue-green algae.

Two cautions. Murkier water has more of everything: with turbidity held constant, the correlation falls from 0.63 to 0.30. And two of the things I tried against the boat data showed nothing: a floating-algae index on the same images (−0.17), and same-day images from the coarser MODIS satellites.

How it was measured
  1. Images. Every Sentinel-2 pass over the lake from June through September, 2017 to 2026, mosaicked by day in Google Earth Engine. Clouds are masked with Cloud Score+, and only pixels that are water at least 80% of the time are kept.
  2. Chlorophyll-a. The Normalized Difference Chlorophyll Index, from the red-edge and red bands, converted to mg/m³ with the Mishra and Mishra (2012) formula for turbid, productive water.
  3. Clear days. A day counts when at least half the lake is cloud-free. That leaves 250 days.
  4. Shores. The median in a 2 km strip along the east shore and the same along the west, from Taycheedah to just below Neenah.
  5. Wind. ERA5-Land daily wind, averaged over the lake and a 10 km ring of shore for the image day and the two days before.
  6. Boat comparison. A third Earth Engine script reads Sentinel-2 under each 60 m square of the USGS boat track. Nothing is cloud-masked in the script; the cloud score is exported and the cut is made afterwards.
  7. Monthly record. The open-source Lake Chlorophyll-a Monitor by Parisa Abbasalizadehrezaei, set up for Lake Winnebago.
What this does not show
  • Not the mats. The index measures chlorophyll in open water. The thick floating mats I remember are a different target, the formula is unreliable on them, and the floating-algae index I tried did not pick them up.
  • Not which algae, directly. Every kind of algae has chlorophyll-a, and Sentinel-2 has no band for the pigment that marks blue-green algae. The boat comparison says the two go together on this lake, but only on eight dates. NOAA monitors this lake with Sentinel-3, which has that band.
  • Not movement. Images are two or more days apart. The result is where chlorophyll sits after the wind, not a bloom tracked across the lake.
  • Clear days only. Clear weather comes with its own typical winds, and neighboring days are not independent, so 250 days is fewer than 250 separate tests.
  • Land-tuned correction. The images use the standard atmospheric correction, which is built for land and less accurate over dark water.
  • Few east-shore measurements. None of the three DNR stations is near the east shore. The USGS boat ran within about a mile of it, on a handful of dates.
