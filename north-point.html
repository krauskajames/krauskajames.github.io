3D capture Drone photogrammetry · Gaussian splatting Milwaukee · October 2026

Milwaukee from the air, in the round

Four interactive 3D models built from drone video: a single landmark, the North Point Water Tower; a whole city block, the St. Mary's Hospital campus; Calatrava's winged pavilion at the Milwaukee Art Museum; and The Couture, a lakefront high-rise. Turn them, zoom in, and look from any side.

Rendered live in your browser with three.js and Spark

What it is

A Gaussian splat stores a scene as millions of tiny, soft, coloured blobs instead of a mesh of triangles. Together they reproduce what the camera saw, including fine detail like carved stone, rooftop equipment and reflections on glass, that a traditional photogrammetry mesh tends to smooth over.

I'm building this workflow at PX Design, where drone splats of client buildings will sit alongside site plans so building managers can inspect a property from their desk.

How it was made
  1. Fly. Orbits at several heights around each site on October 29, 2026, with exposure locked so every frame shares the same brightness.
  2. Check the logs. Read the drone's per-frame flight logs (.SRT) to confirm the passes close around the subject and sit at different heights before processing anything.
  3. Extract frames. Pull a few hundred stills from the video with ffmpeg and drop blurry ones.
  4. Solve the cameras. COLMAP matches features across every image and works out where each was taken, producing a sparse point cloud. Lens distortion is then removed so the images suit the splat trainer.
  5. Train the splat. Brush fits millions of Gaussians to the images from those camera positions.
  6. Clean and publish. Crop to the site in SuperSplat, filter out floaters with a short Python script, compress with splat-transform, and render it here with Spark.

Problems worth solving

  • Ghost copies. Repetitive features like rows of identical windows can fool the camera solve into placing a second, smaller copy of a building. Stricter registration thresholds and matching settings fixed it.
  • Coverage, not frame count, decides quality. Several orbits at different heights beat one long pass. I now check the flight logs before processing to catch gaps early.
  • Mixing cameras. Ground photos fill in what a drone sees poorly, but only if COLMAP treats them as a second camera with its own lens. One shared lens model bends the reconstruction.
  • Floaters and fringe. Stray Gaussians in the sky and large faint ones along the crop edge show up as haze. A short filter on height, size and opacity removed about a thousand of them from the hospital model without touching the buildings.
  • Leveling. A model can come out of training slightly tilted. I measure a tower's centerline at several heights and rotate it upright; The Couture was 3.1° off and is now within 0.1°.
  • Web delivery. The tower's trained splat was 458 MB. Cropping and compression brought the four models to between 1.8 MB and 13.5 MB, small enough to load in seconds on a phone.
